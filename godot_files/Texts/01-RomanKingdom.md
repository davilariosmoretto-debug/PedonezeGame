<h1>The Founding of Rome</h1>
<p>Roman tradition places the founding of the city in 753 BC, on the <b>Palatine Hill</b> beside the <b>Tiber</b>. Its founder was <b>Romulus</b>, one of twin brothers born, the story says, to the god Mars and raised by a she-wolf. The tale belongs to Rome’s oldest traditions; the beginnings of the settlement are not known with certainty.</p>
<p>Romulus and his brother <b>Remus</b> disputed where their city should stand. Remus was killed, and Romulus gave the settlement his own name. The story remembers both the founding of a community and the violence said to have attended its birth.</p>

<h1>The Romans and the Sabines</h1>
<p>In the traditional account, Romulus gathered newcomers into the city, but Rome’s growth brought conflict with its neighbors. The best-known story concerns the <b>Sabines</b>, whose people were drawn into war with the Romans after a gathering at which Sabine women were seized.</p>
<p>The fighting ended, the story says, when the women intervened between their Roman husbands and their Sabine kin. The two peoples made peace and joined their communities. Roman tradition thus explained the city’s early growth as the joining of neighboring peoples, though the details belong to legend.</p>

<h1>The Kings and the Early City</h1>
<p>After Romulus, Roman tradition names <b>Numa Pompilius</b>, remembered for establishing religious rites and priesthoods. The next kings were <b>Tullus Hostilius</b>, associated with war against Alba Longa, and <b>Ancus Marcius</b>, remembered for strengthening the city and its connections to the coast.</p>
<p>The old accounts also describe a <b>Senate</b> of leading men who advised the king, alongside assemblies of the people. These stories connect Rome’s earliest institutions with its kings, but they were written down long after the kingdom’s supposed beginnings, and cannot all be confirmed.</p>

<h1>The Later Kings and the End of the Monarchy</h1>
<p>The last part of the traditional king-list includes <b>Tarquinius Priscus</b>, <b>Servius Tullius</b>, and Tarquinius Superbus. Roman accounts associate these reigns with building works, changes to the organization of the people, and growing Etruscan influence. The sequence is part of Rome’s received history, though the details remain uncertain.</p>
<p>Tarquinius Superbus was remembered as a harsh ruler. According to tradition, his family’s conduct brought revolt, and the king was driven from Rome in 509 BC. The monarchy ended and the <b>Republic</b> began—an event Romans later treated as a warning against rule by a king.</p>
